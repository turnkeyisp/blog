# Choosing a US-Based ISP Call Center

An ISP selecting a call center needs to know who will answer subscriber calls, what those staff can do, and how unresolved work reaches the local team. A company's address answers only part of that question.

TurnkeyISP was founded in Santa Fe, New Mexico and works with an English-speaking team in the United States and around the world. A US business address does not mean every agent is physically located in the United States. Discuss any location requirements explicitly when defining your service.

![US locations](/blog/static/images/us-locations.jpg)

## Start with the subscriber's support needs

An inbound queue may include a disconnected subscriber, a billing question, or a prospective customer asking about service availability. Define which inquiries the remote team should handle and which require an authorized local contact.

For [WISP technical support](https://turnkeyisp.com/wisp-support/), discuss the equipment and systems staff will use to distinguish a home Wi-Fi issue from an access-network problem. Review a troubleshooting workflow and the notes your team expects to receive with an escalation.

Ask about:

- The subscriber information staff may access and the identity checks they should perform.
- The approved troubleshooting steps and remote actions.
- How sales and billing questions reach the appropriate person.
- Which incidents need an immediate call to your on-call staff.
- What information is recorded before a field visit is requested.

## Compare shared coverage with a dedicated role

A shared call center and a dedicated staff member meet different operating needs. Shared coverage handles an agreed queue and coverage window. A dedicated role focuses on your company during its agreed schedule and can include ongoing ticket follow-up and administrative work.

If your local office handles daytime calls, [after-hours and overflow support](https://turnkeyisp.com/after-hours-isp-support/) can be scoped around evenings, weekends, or a busy queue. If you need continuity in one company's daily workflow, review [dedicated ISP staffing](https://turnkeyisp.com/dedicated-isp-staffing/).

A single dedicated role should not be treated as a complete 24/7 staffing plan. Specify the hours, handoffs, and coverage required when that role is unavailable.

## Separate subscriber support from network operations

Answering a subscriber call and investigating an infrastructure alarm are related responsibilities, but they need different runbooks and permissions. A call-handling team needs current outage information. Network operations staff need defined monitoring scope, escalation priorities, carrier contacts, and authorization for any remote actions.

Review [ISP NOC monitoring and support](https://turnkeyisp.com/isp-noc-services/) separately if you need alert investigation and outage coordination. Engineering changes and physical repairs should follow the approval and dispatch processes you establish.

## Review communication and system access

Evaluate how clearly staff communicate, document a problem, and explain the next step. Ask how they learn your equipment, subscriber systems, and support procedures. Physical location alone does not establish technical ability or familiarity with your ISP.

Provide access appropriate to the role, identify who can approve changes, and define how access is removed when responsibilities change. Keep the support runbook available to both the remote team and the local staff receiving escalations.

## Agree on the handoff and reporting workflow

Decide which system contains the authoritative ticket record. A useful handoff identifies the symptoms, checks completed, actions taken, unresolved questions, and next owner. Agree how the next shift receives open work and how current outage information reaches subscriber-facing staff.

Review support activity using the records and definitions agreed for your service. If you track call volume, unresolved tickets, or escalation reasons, confirm how each is recorded before comparing periods or teams.

## Prepare for onboarding

Before an introduction, gather your required coverage hours, queue-routing rules, systems list, access requirements, and escalation contacts. Identify the tasks your local team wants help with and those that must remain with local engineers or field technicians.
